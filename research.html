Board Gender Quotas and Career Spillovers in Banking

Job Market Paper · with Shuo Xia

This paper examines whether and how board gender quotas reshape women’s careers across the bank leadership hierarchy. Using a large individual–bank–year panel covering listed banks in Europe and North America over 2000–2021, we exploit the staggered adoption of binding quotas across European countries in a difference-in-differences design. We find that quotas increase women’s board representation, with limited representation spillovers to non-board senior managers. They nevertheless generate career-flow spillovers: turnover declines for female supervisory directors, transitions from senior management to executive-director roles increase for women, and departing female executive directors are less likely to secure another executive-director appointment.

Stability versus Disruption in the Market for Bank Executives

with Stefano Colonnello and Shuo Xia

This paper examines how bank leadership and executive labor markets evolved amid regulatory, macroeconomic, and technological change. Using a director–bank–year panel covering listed banks in Europe and North America over 2000–2021, we find that executive boards remained broadly stable: turnover rose around the Global Financial Crisis but showed no sustained upward trend, board composition changed gradually, and careers after turnover remained concentrated in banking and senior roles. Regression results show that executive centrality predicts retention, while career capital shapes subsequent mobility: larger networks predict post-turnover employment, broader experience predicts reallocation across sectors and countries, and internal appointments favor managerial depth over industry breadth.

Banking Transformation Since 2000: A Literature Review and Stylized Facts from Listed Banks

This paper documents how banking has changed using a panel of listed banks in Europe and North America over 2000–2021. Banks became larger, less branch-intensive, and better capitalized. Although net interest margins narrowed substantially, loans and customer deposits remained central and the non-interest income share did not rise, indicating adaptation within the traditional intermediation model.
